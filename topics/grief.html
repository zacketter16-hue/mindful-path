2 Understanding the Grieving Process

Grief Is a Response to Change

Loss creates a gap between the life you expected or became accustomed to and the life that now exists.

Your mind may still expect the person to walk through the door, call your phone, sit in their usual chair, or be present at an important event. You may automatically reach for your phone to contact them before remembering that you cannot.

This can make grief feel disorienting.

Part of you understands what happened intellectually, while another part has not fully adjusted emotionally. This is why grief can feel surreal, especially soon after a loss. People may describe feeling numb, detached, or as though they are watching their life from the outside.

Numbness can be the mind’s way of protecting itself from absorbing more pain than it can process at once.

Grief is not simply an emotional reaction. It is the mind and body learning how to exist in a reality that no longer includes something important.

Grief is a significant response to a huge, permanent life change. Every emotion is valid and on the table.

The Many Emotions of Grief

Grief is often portrayed as overwhelming sadness, but the emotional experience is usually more complicated.

You may experience:

Sadness, of course

Sadness may reflect longing, loneliness, and recognition of what has been lost.

Anger

You may feel angry at the person who died, medical professionals, family members, yourself, a higher power, or life itself.

Anger may also emerge because the loss feels unfair or because you had no control over what happened.

Guilt

You may replay past conversations or decisions:

“I should have called more.”

“I wish our last conversation had been different.”

“Why am I still here when they are not?”

Some guilt reflects genuine regret, but grief can also create an exaggerated sense of responsibility for events that were never really within your control.

Anxiety

Loss can make the world feel less predictable.

After something painful happens, you may become more afraid that another loss will occur. You may worry excessively about your health, the safety of loved ones, or your ability to cope with future challenges.

Relief

Relief can occur after a long illness, caregiving burden, painful relationship, or prolonged period of uncertainty.

Feeling relief doesn’t mean you didn’t love and care the person or value the relationship. Multiple emotions can exist at the same time.

Numbness

Some people feel very little at first. Others move between intense emotion and emotional emptiness.

There is no required emotional response that proves the loss mattered. No emotion is right or wrong. Again, every emotion is valid and on the table.

Grief Can Affect the Body Physically

Grief is not experienced only through thoughts and feelings. It can produce physical symptoms similar to anxiety such as:

  • Fatigue
  • Tightness in the chest
  • Stomach discomfort
  • Headaches
  • Muscle tension
  • Changes in appetite
  • Difficulty sleeping
  • Sleeping much more than usual
  • Restlessness
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • A sensation of heaviness
  • Increased sensitivity to noise or stimulation

People sometimes worry that they are becoming physically ill or losing control of themselves.

Like anxiety, while physical symptoms should be evaluated when they are severe, persistent, or concerning, many bodily changes can occur because grief activates the stress response and disrupts normal routines.

Like depression, basic tasks may temporarily require much more effort, and a general heaviness and sadness can feel consuming.

Remember, your mind and body are using significant energy to process a major change.

There Is No Universal Grief Timeline (It takes what it takes)

People often want to know:

“How long will this last?”

But there is no universal answer.

The intensity and duration of grief can be influenced by:

  • Whether the loss was sudden or expected
  • The closeness of the relationship
  • Previous losses or trauma
  • Available support
  • Circumstances surrounding the loss
  • Ongoing legal, medical, or financial stress
  • Cultural and spiritual beliefs
  • Mental and physical health
  • Whether the relationship contained unresolved conflict

Because of all the factors, people can and will experience the same loss very differently. Members of the same family will grieve in different ways. One person may want to talk constantly, while another becomes quiet and wants to be left alone. One may return to work quickly, while another struggles to function. One may cry frequently, while another doesn’t outwardly express emotions.

Different grieving styles don’t represent love and care for the person who passed, and comparing your grief with someone else’s creates unnecessary shame and conflict at a time that demands compassion and connection.

The Misunderstanding of the Five Stages

Some of you may be familiar with the five stages commonly associated with grief:

  • Denial
  • Anger
  • Bargaining
  • Depression
  • Acceptance

These ideas can help describe some common reactions to loss, but they are often misunderstood as a fixed sequence that everyone must complete.

Grief rarely works that neatly.

You may experience several reactions in the same day. You may feel accepting one week and deeply angry the next. You may never strongly identify with one or more of the stages. It’s all on the table. It’s all valid. There’s no order, rhyme, or reason to it.

A more accurate way to think of grief is a collection of emotional experiences that may appear, disappear, overlap, and return over time.

Acceptance also does not mean approval, or believing the loss was fair. It just means gradually recognizing that the loss has objectively occurred, that there’s nothing you can do about it, and learning how to live within that reality.

Grief Comes in Waves

Early grief may feel constant. Over time, it often begins to arrive and leave in waves.

A person may feel relatively stable and then suddenly become triggered and overwhelmed by:

  • A song
  • A smell
  • A picture
  • A familiar location
  • A holiday
  • A birthday
  • An anniversary
  • A major life event
  • Something they wish they could share with the person

These experiences can be surprising and discouraging.

You may think:

“I thought I was doing better.”

But a difficult moment does not erase previous progress. Healing is not a straight line in which each day feels slightly better than the day before.

A more useful way to evaluate progress (with grief and with almost all mental health treatment) is to observe whether painful emotional waves are gradually changing in:

Frequency

Are they happening less often?

Severity

Are they becoming less overwhelming when they occur?

Duration

Are you recovering more quickly afterward?

You may still have extremely difficult days. The larger question is whether, over time, the grief is allowing more room for other experiences.

Different Types of Grief

Understanding the form a loss takes can help explain why some experiences are especially difficult.

Anticipatory Grief

Anticipatory grief occurs when someone begins grieving before the loss fully happens.

This may occur during terminal illness, progressive disease, declining independence, or an expected separation.

The person may grieve:

  • What has already changed
  • What they expect to lose
  • Future experiences that may never happen

Anticipatory grief doesn’t eliminate grief after the loss, but it may prepare someone in certain ways for what’s to come.

Ambiguous Loss

Ambiguous loss occurs when there is no clear or complete ending.

Examples may include:

  • A missing person
  • Dementia or severe cognitive decline
  • Estrangement
  • A loved one who is physically present but psychologically unavailable
  • A relationship that ends without explanation

Ambiguous loss can be especially difficult because the mind does not receive a clear conclusion. Hope, grief, uncertainty, and confusion may coexist.

Disenfranchised Grief

Disenfranchised grief occurs when a loss is not fully known by, recognized, or supported by others.

Examples may include:

  • Miscarriage
  • The death of an ex-partner
  • The death of a pet
  • Loss of a secret or complicated relationship
  • Estrangement
  • Job or identity loss

When other people minimize the loss, the grieving person may feel isolated or believe they are not entitled to their emotions.

A loss does not have to be widely understood to be psychologically significant.

Grief Is Not the Same as Depression

Grief and depression can share symptoms, including sadness, disrupted sleep, low motivation, difficulty concentrating, and reduced interest in life. But they are not always the same experience.

Grief often comes in waves and is closely connected to thoughts or reminders of the loss. Even during grief, a person may still experience moments of connection, humor, affection, or hope.

Depression may create a more persistent sense of emptiness, worthlessness, hopelessness, or disconnection that extends beyond the loss itself.

Grief can also contribute to depression, especially when symptoms are severe, prolonged, or significantly impair functioning.

Additional support may be needed when someone experiences:

  • Persistent inability to function
  • Severe hopelessness
  • Intense self-blame
  • Significant substance use
  • Ongoing inability to care for basic needs
  • Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
  • Grief that remains intensely disabling over a prolonged period

Seeking support does not mean someone is grieving incorrectly. It means the pain deserves care.

Final Thoughts

Grief is not a problem to solve as quickly as possible. It’s a normal, understandable human response to the loss of someone, something, or some version of life that mattered to you.

You may not be able to control when grief appears or how strongly it affects you. But understanding the grieving process can reduce the fear that something is wrong with you.

Grief can be unpredictable. It can involve contradictory emotions. It can affect the mind and body. It may return during important moments years later.

None of this means you are failing to heal. The goal is to gradually learn how to carry the loss while allowing life to grow around it.

Key Takeaways

  • Grief can follow death, relationship loss, illness, job loss, loss of identity, and many other significant changes.
  • Grief may involve sadness, anger, guilt, anxiety, relief, numbness, and physical symptoms.
  • There is no universal grieving style or timeline.
  • Grief often comes in waves, and triggers do not mean that progress has been erased.
  • Frequency, severity, and duration can help measure change over time.
  • Acceptance means recognizing reality, not approving of what happened.
  • Healing does not mean forgetting. It means gradually adapting to life after loss.

🧠 Your Mental Playbook

One Thing to Remember

Grief is not a sequence you complete, it’s a process of adapting to a reality that you didn’t choose and learning to find meaning and purpose in your life after the loss.

You are not failing because your emotions are unpredictable, because a reminder affects you unexpectedly, or because your grief looks different from someone else’s.

Reflect

Take a moment to consider your experience of loss.

Ask yourself:

  • What have I actually lost beyond the person or event itself?
  • Have I been judging my grief because it does not look the way I expected?
  • Am I comparing my grieving process with someone else’s?
  • What situations, dates, or reminders tend to trigger stronger emotional waves?
  • Over time, have those waves changed in frequency, severity, or duration?

The purpose is to understand what your grief is asking you to acknowledge.

Put It Into Practice

Create a Grief Wave Plan for moments when emotions unexpectedly intensify.

Write down:

1. My common triggers

Examples might include anniversaries, pictures, music, certain locations, holidays, or major life events.

2. What grief feels like in my body

You may notice chest tightness, fatigue, restlessness, nausea, numbness, or difficulty concentrating.

3. What I tend to need

This may include:

  • Time alone
  • Contact with someone supportive
  • Movement or fresh air
  • A meaningful ritual
  • Journaling
  • Permission and space to cry
  • A temporary break from responsibilities

4. What I can remind myself

Choose a statement such as:

“This is a wave. It is painful, but it will give way to calmer waters soon.”

“A difficult moment does not erase my progress.”

“I can miss what I lost and still continue living.”

5. Who I can contact

Identify at least one person, therapist, support group, or crisis resource you can reach when grief feels too heavy to carry alone.

3 The Need for Answers and Meaning

Why the Mind Searches for Answers

As discussed extensively in the Anxiety Playbook, the human brain is uncomfortable with uncertainty.

When something significant happens, the mind tries to identify a cause:

  • What led to this?
  • Who was responsible?
  • What warning signs were missed?
  • What could have changed the outcome?
  • How can this be prevented from happening again?

This process in everyday life is often useful. We want to learn from mistakes, apply new perspective, and get a better outcome next time.

But grief often turns reflection into rumination. Instead of producing useful insight, the same questions repeat:

“What if I had called?”

“What if we had gone to another doctor?”

“Should I have called more?”

The mind may believe that if it reviews the situation enough times, it will eventually discover the explanation that makes everything feel better. Rumination can create the feeling that you are solving the loss while actually keeping you emotionally trapped inside it.

Sometimes there are no answers. And even if there was, what would they change?

The Search for Control

Many grief-related questions are attempts to recover a sense of control.

If you can identify exactly why something happened, you may feel more capable of preventing another painful event. If you can find the mistake, you may feel that the world is still understandable.

This is one reason self-blame can become so powerful after loss. Strangely, blaming yourself may sometimes feel less frightening than accepting that certain events were unpredictable or beyond your control.

The belief:

“I should have prevented this”

can create enormous guilt. But it may also preserve the illusion that terrible outcomes are preventable.

Recognizing the limits of your control can be painful because it means acknowledging that love, effort, attention, and good intentions cannot protect us from every loss.

Guilt and the Benefit of Hindsight

After a loss, people often review the past using information they didn’t have at the time. This is called hindsight bias.

Once you know the outcome, earlier moments may suddenly appear obvious:

“I should have known.”

“The signs were right there.”

“I should have made a different decision.”

But the situation wasn’t clear while it was happening. You were making decisions based on:

  • What you knew at the time
  • What professionals or others told you
  • The options available
  • Your emotional and physical capacity

A useful question is:

“Am I judging my past self using information that only became clear afterward?”

You can acknowledge genuine regret without assuming complete responsibility for everything that occurred.

Real Guilt vs. Grief Guilt

Not all guilt is the same. Sometimes people regret something they actually said, did, or failed to do. Honest accountability can be part of healing. You may wish you had apologized, spent more time with someone, handled a conflict differently, or been more emotionally present.

This can be thought of as real guilt: regret connected to a specific behavior that you recognize did not align with your values.

Grief can also produce guilt that exceeds your actual responsibility.

Examples include:

  • Feeling responsible for another person’s illness
  • Believing you should have predicted an unpredictable event
  • Believing you should have been present when your life required you to be elsewhere
  • Believing you should have somehow convinced someone to make a different decision
  • Feeling guilty for surviving
  • Feeling guilty when you laugh, enjoy yourself, or begin moving forward

This is often grief guilt: the mind’s attempt to explain the loss, maintain a connection, or find control in a situation where control was limited or impossible.

Both forms of guilt deserve compassion.

Unfinished Conversations

Loss can leave people with thoughts and emotions they never had the opportunity to express.

You may wish you had said:

  • “I love you.”
  • “I forgive you.”
  • “I’m sorry.”
  • “Thank you.”
  • “You hurt me.”
  • “I understand now.”
  • “I wish things had been different.”

The relationship may have ended suddenly, or it may have been complicated long before the loss occurred.

People sometimes assume that grief should be easier when the relationship was difficult. In reality, complicated relationships may create more unanswered questions because the opportunity for repair is gone.

You may be grieving both:

  • The relationship you had
  • The relationship you hoped you might eventually have

Closure does not always require a final conversation with the person. Realistically, may need other ways to express what remains unsaid through:

  • Writing a letter
  • Journaling
  • Prayer or spiritual practice
  • Therapy
  • A personal ritual
  • A conversation with someone who understood the relationship

These practices can’t change the past, but they can help give unfinished thoughts and emotions somewhere to go.

When There Is No Satisfying Explanation

Some losses remain forever unclear.

A death may occur without warning. A medical outcome may have been highly unlikely or difficult to explain. Someone may leave without saying goodbye or providing a reason. A loved one may die by suicide or overdose, leaving family members with gut-wrenching questions about what they knew, felt, or intended.

Even when an official explanation exists, it may not answer the emotional question:

“Why did this have to happen?”

This is one of the hardest realities of grief:

An explanation does not necessarily lead to emotional peace.

You may receive answers that create new questions. You may hear an explanation that’s not good enough, and still feel that it’s unfair, incomplete, or impossible to accept.

Healing may require accepting not the loss itself, but the possibility that some questions will remain unanswered.

The Difference Between Answers and Meaning

Answers attempt to explain the past. Meaning helps us decide how to live in the future.

An answer asks:

“Why did this happen?”

Meaning asks:

“How will I carry this experience forward?”

Meaning doesn’t require believing that the loss happened for a good reason. Statements such as “everything happens for a reason” can be painful and invalidating, particularly after traumatic or preventable loss.

Some events are tragic, unfair, and should never have happened. Creating meaning doesn’t mean justifying the loss. It means choosing what you will do with the life and love that remain.

Meaning might involve:

  • Living according to values the person taught you
  • Supporting others who experience a similar loss
  • Creating a tradition in someone’s memory
  • Becoming more present and appreciative of life
  • Advocating for change
  • Allowing the loss to clarify what matters most

Meaning isn’t found in the suffering itself. It can be created through how you respond to it.

Continuing Bonds

Older ideas about grief sometimes suggested that people needed to detach from those who we’ve lost in order to move forward. Today, many people understand the importance of continuing bonds.

You may continue the bond by:

  • Telling stories of the person you lost
  • Keeping photographs or meaningful objects to look at when it’s helpful to
  • Following traditions you did together
  • Visiting a meaningful place
  • Cooking a meal you enjoyed with them
  • Considering what advice the person might have offered
  • Living out values you learned from them

This is not the same as denying that the loss occurred or living in denial. It’s recognizing that someone can remain meaningful even though they are no longer physically present.

This is simply finding a way for the relationship to exist within your new reality.

Faith, Spirituality, and Meaning

Loss can strengthen, challenge, or completely change a person’s spiritual beliefs.

Some people find comfort in prayer, faith communities, rituals, beliefs about an afterlife, or the idea that the person remains spiritually present. Others feel angry, betrayed, or abandoned by their faith.

They may ask:

“How could God have allowed this to happen?”

“Why weren’t my prayers answered?”

There is no required spiritual response to grief. Doubt does not make you disloyal. Anger does not mean your faith has disappeared. And finding comfort in your faith does not mean the loss hurts less or that you’re taking the easy way out.

Spiritual questions may take time, and they tend not to end in certainty. A compassionate spiritual leader, therapist, or trusted community may help someone explore these questions without forcing simple explanations onto a complicated loss.

Key Takeaways

  • The mind searches for answers after loss to restore order, predictability, and control.
  • Reflection can create insight, but rumination often leads to more questions without resolution.
  • Hindsight may make past decisions appear clearer than they were at the time.
  • Genuine regret can require accountability to heal, while grief guilt may lead to an unfair sense of responsibility.
  • An explanation does not always create emotional peace.
  • Finding meaning does not require believing the loss happened for a good reason.
  • Continuing bonds allow an important relationship to remain part of your life in a new form.
  • Faith may comfort, challenge, or change during grief.

🧠 Your Mental Playbook

One Thing to Remember

Even if you could get them, reasons and explanations would likely not be enough, and wouldn’t change the outcome. But you can eventually decide what the loss will mean in the life you continue to live.

Unanswered questions do not have to control the rest of your story.

Reflect

Consider the questions you have been carrying since the loss.

Ask yourself:

  • Is this question helping me understand something, or keeping me trapped in the same moment?
  • Am I judging myself with information I did not have at the time?
  • What responsibility was truly mine, and what was outside my control?
  • Have I been pressuring myself to find a lesson or purpose before I am ready?
  • What values, memories, or qualities from this relationship do I want to carry forward?

You don’t need to answer everything today. Sometimes healing begins by asking whether a question can be answered, not by demanding that it must be.

Put It Into Practice

Write a letter titled: “What I know, What I don’t know, and What I Will Carry Forward”

Divide it into three sections.

1. What I Know

Write down the facts you can reasonably accept.

  • What happened objectively
  • What you did with the information you had
  • What the relationship meant to you
  • What you appreciated
  • What, if anything, you regret

2. What I May Never Know

Name the unanswered questions you have been carrying.

You are not giving up on understanding. You are acknowledging that repeatedly demanding an answer may be costing you more than it provides.

Finish this section with:

“I do not need to solve every unanswered question in order to continue healing.”

3. What I Will Carry Forward

Identify what you want to preserve from the person, relationship, or experience.

  • A value
  • A memory
  • A tradition
  • A cause you care about
  • A promise to live more fully
  • A commitment to seek help or offer it

Meaning is not always something you discover hidden inside the loss. Sometimes it’s something you create through the life you build afterward.

“When we are no longer able to change a situation, we are challenged to change ourselves.” — Viktor E. Frankl
