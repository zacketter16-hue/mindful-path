2 Building Confidence Through Action

Introduction

You might think confidence needs to come before action.

People tell themselves:

“Once I feel more confident, I’ll speak up.”

“Once I stop doubting myself, I’ll apply.”

“Once I feel ready, I’ll take the risk.”

But confidence develops in the opposite direction. You act first, then your mind begins collecting evidence that you can handle the situation.

This is why waiting to feel confident (similar to motivation) is a trap. If confidence depends on experience, but you avoid the experiences that would help build it, confidence has no opportunity to grow.

Confidence Is Built Through Evidence

Confidence is not simply positive thinking, it’s built from a collection of evidence your mind has stored.

If you repeatedly avoid difficult situations, your mind may begin to conclude:

“I probably can’t handle this.”

But if you repeatedly enter difficult situations, make mistakes, adjust, and continue, a different belief can begin to form:

“This may be uncomfortable, but I can work through it.”

That’s a more durable, real form of confidence.

Every time you:

  • Speak even though you feel nervous
  • Try something unfamiliar or uncomfortable
  • Set a boundary
  • Complete a difficult task
  • Recover after a mistake
  • Follow through on a commitment
  • Return after a setback

you give yourself another piece of evidence.

Confidence isn’t just about your ability to succeed, it’s about the evidence you accumulate that you can try, adapt, learn, and continue on.

Confidence Doesn’t Mean Feeling Certain

One of the biggest misconceptions about confidence is that confident people feel sure of themselves all the time.

They do not.

Confidence can exist alongside anxiety, doubt, nervousness, and discomfort.

You can be nervous before a presentation and still speak effectively.

You can question whether you are ready for an opportunity and still pursue it.

You can feel intimidated in a new environment and still participate.

The goal is not:

“I must eliminate doubt before I act.”

A better goal is:

“I can act even while doubt is present.”

That distinction matters because uncertainty and discomfort are unavoidable whenever you are doing something meaningful, unfamiliar, or challenging.

Be Task-Oriented

Competence Can Create Confidence

Sometimes low confidence is not just a mindset problem. Sometimes you simply need more practice.

If you have never performed a skill before, uncertainty is reasonable. Confidence can increase as competence increases.

That may require:

  • Education
  • Repetition
  • Coaching
  • Feedback
  • Preparation

Someone learning public speaking may become more confident because they’ve practiced dozens of times.

Someone starting a new job may feel more confident after learning the systems.

Someone beginning strength training may become more comfortable after learning proper technique.

You don’t need to believe in your ability when it’s unsupported by evidence. But your belief if your ability to improve over time through reps and experience can give you the confidence you need to continue.

Keep Promises to Yourself

Confidence is influenced by self-trust.

When you repeatedly tell yourself you’ll do something and consistently avoid it, your mind notices.

When you follow through, even in small ways, you create a different pattern.

For example:

  • You said you would exercise for fifteen minutes, and you did.
  • You said you would send the email, and you sent it.
  • You said you would have the conversation, and you had it.
  • You said you would restart after missing a day, and you restarted.

These actions may appear small from the outside, but internally, they communicate an important message:

“I can rely on myself.”

That sense of self-trust and the habit of following through is an important foundation for confidence.

Stop Using Feelings as the Requirement

If your rule is:

“I will do it when I feel confident,”

you may wait indefinitely.

Feelings make for good consultants, but terrible CEOs. They should be used as guidance and information, not as decision makers.

You can feel afraid and still move forward.

You can feel awkward and still practice.

You can feel unmotivated and still begin.

You can feel uncertain and still make a thoughtful decision.

This is one of the most important shifts in building confidence. As we’ve discussed in several MFL Playbooks: the behavior comes first, the feeling catches up later.

Key Takeaways

  • Confidence isn’t necessary to act, it develops afterwards.
  • Experience gives your mind evidence that you can cope, learn, and adapt.
  • Task orientation allows you to focus on controllable actions, not just outcomes.
  • Sometimes greater competence, preparation, and practice are needed.
  • Following through on commitments can strengthen self-trust.

🧠 Your Mental Playbook

One Thing to Remember

You don’t become confident by with time or patience. You become more confident by collecting evidence that you can act before you feel completely ready.

Reflect

Think of one area where you want greater confidence.

Ask yourself:

  • What am I afraid might happen?
  • Am I focusing more on how I will be judged than on the task itself?
  • What skill or preparation would increase my competence?
  • What small action would give me useful evidence?
  • What promise to myself could I follow through on this week?

Put It Into Practice

Create a Confidence Repetition.

Choose one situation where you usually hesitate.

1. Define the task

What specifically needs to be done?

“Speak once during the meeting.”

2. Reduce the challenge

Make the step small enough that you are willing to attempt it.

3. Act before you feel completely ready

Complete the behavior while allowing discomfort to be present.

4. Record the evidence

Afterward, write:

  • What did I do?
  • What did I handle?
  • What did I learn?
  • What would I try next time?

“You don’t need to feel fearless to become more confident. You need to prove to yourself that fear doesn’t have to make your decisions.” — Mental Fitness Lounge
