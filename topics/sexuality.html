2 Understanding Sexuality and Sexual Orientation

What Is Sexuality?

Sexuality is broadly how people experience and understand attraction, intimacy, relationships, and sexual expression.

It may include:

  • Sexual attraction
  • Romantic attraction
  • Emotional connection
  • Physical affection
  • Sexual desire
  • Sexual behavior
  • Relationship preferences
  • Values and beliefs about sex
  • Sexual orientation
  • Sexual identity

Someone might experience attraction without wanting a relationship. Someone might desire romantic intimacy without experiencing much sexual attraction. Another might experience sexual attraction but decide not to act on it because of personal values, safety, religion, relationship commitments, or life circumstances.

Sexuality isn’t always just what someone does, it includes what they feel, how they connect, what they value, and how they understand those experiences.

What Is Sexual Orientation?

Sexual orientation refers to someone’s romantic and sexual attraction toward people of a particular sex/gender.

Common orientation labels include:

Heterosexual or straight

A person who is primarily attracted to people of the opposite different sex/gender.

Gay

A person who is primarily attracted to others of the same sex/gender.

Lesbian

A woman who is primarily attracted to women.

Bisexual

A person who experiences attraction to more than one sex/gender.

Bisexuality doesn’t mean equal attraction to each sex/gender. Attraction may differ in intensity, frequency, or form.

Pansexual

A person whose attraction is not limited by sex/gender – someone who doesn’t find sex/gender as a primary factor in attraction.

Asexual

A person who experiences little or no sexual attraction.

Asexual people can still experience romantic attraction, emotional intimacy, physical affection, or a desire for relationships.

Queer

A broad term some people use when their sexuality doesn’t fit heterosexual expectations or when they prefer not to use a more specific label. Some people embrace this term, while others struggle with its historic use as a slur.

These terms are useful, but they shouldn’t be treated like diagnostic categories. People may understand or use them somewhat differently based on their culture, generation, community, or personal experience.

Attraction Is Not One Single Experience

Attraction can take several forms.

Sexual Attraction

Sexual attraction involves feeling sexually drawn to another person or desiring sexual contact with them. It’s different from simply recognizing that someone is physically attractive. You may find someone aesthetically appealing without wanting sexual intimacy with them.

Romantic Attraction

Romantic attraction may involve wanting:

  • Dating
  • Partnership
  • Emotional closeness
  • Romantic affection
  • Commitment
  • A shared life

Sexual and romantic attraction often occur together, but they don’t have to.

Emotional Attraction

Emotional attraction involves feeling drawn to someone’s personality, character, intelligence, humor, or emotional presence. It can contribute to friendship, romance, or sexual intimacy.

Recognizing these differences can help people understand why their feelings don’t always fit a simple formula.

You may feel emotionally close to someone without wanting sex. You could experience sexual attraction without imagining a relationship. You can want affection without experiencing either romantic or sexual attraction.

None of these experiences automatically invalidate or require the others.

Sexual Orientation, Behavior, and Identity

One of the most important distinctions in understanding sexuality is the difference between:

  • Attraction
  • Behavior
  • Identity

These three areas often overlap, but they’re not identical.

Attraction

Attraction refers to the feelings a person experiences.

Behavior

Behavior refers to what someone has or has not done.

Identity

Identity refers to the language someone uses to understand and describe their sexuality.

For example:

  • A bisexual person may have only dated one gender.
  • A gay person may previously have had relationships with someone of the opposite sex.
  • Someone may experience same-sex attraction without identifying as gay or bisexual.
  • Someone may engage in a sexual behavior without feeling that a corresponding identity label suits them.

Dating and sexual history don’t always tell the whole story. People may make relationship choices because of:

  • Opportunity
  • Social expectations
  • Family pressure
  • Religion
  • Safety
  • Fear of rejection
  • A lack of self-understanding
  • A desire for privacy

Sexuality vs. Gender Identity

Sexual orientation and gender identity are related concepts, but they’re not the same.

Gender identity is a person’s internal understanding of their gender, while sexual orientation refers to patterns of attraction.

Both cisgender and transgender people can be straight, gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, asexual, or use another orientation label. Someone’s gender identity does not determine who they’re attracted to.

Understanding this distinction prevents sexuality and gender from being treated as interchangeable.

Labels Can Be Helpful

Sexuality labels can provide:

  • Language
  • Clarity
  • Validation
  • Community
  • Belonging
  • Relief
  • Access to support
  • A way to communicate with partners
  • A sense of pride

For someone who has spent years feeling different or alone, finding a word that reflects their experience can be powerful.

A label may communicate:

“There are other people like me.”

“My experience has a name.”

Labels can help people find communities, resources, relationships, and role models.

Using a label doesn’t mean allowing it to define every aspect of your life. Sexuality can be important without becoming the only important thing about you.

Stability, Fluidity, and Change

Some people experience their sexuality as consistent throughout life.

Others notice changes in:

  • The people they are attracted to
  • The intensity of attraction
  • The way they describe themselves
  • Their comfort with particular labels
  • Their understanding of past experiences
  • Their desire for sex or relationships

Saying that sexuality can be fluid doesn’t mean that everyone’s orientation changes. It also doesn’t mean that sexual orientation can be deliberately changed through pressure, treatment, or willpower.

Some people experience genuine fluidity. Others experience strong stability. A changed label over time doesn’t necessarily mean attraction has changed, more often it’s simply an increase in understanding of feelings that have always been there.

For example, someone may initially identify as straight because heterosexuality was the only possibility they had considered. Later, they may recognize attractions that had previously been minimized, misunderstood, or ignored.

Heteronormativity

Heteronormativity is society’s assumption that heterosexuality is the default, expected, or most natural orientation.

It may appear in subtle ways:

  • Assuming a child will eventually date someone of a different sex/gender
  • Asking a boy whether he has a girlfriend
  • Asking a girl whether she has a boyfriend
  • Presenting only heterosexual relationships in education, media, music, etc.
  • Treating LGB relationships as unusual or requiring explanation
  • Assuming marriage and children will follow one particular pattern
  • Describing someone as straight unless they state otherwise

Growing up, we absorb and internalize these assumptions before understanding our own sexuality. As a result, an LGB person might initially assume they are or should be straight, misinterpret or feel shame about their same-sex attractions, and try to make themselves fit into society’s expected boxes.

Heteronormativity isn’t always intentional prejudice. It describes a social environment where one orientation is treated as standard and others are treated as exceptions.

Culture, Family, Religion, and Media

Sexuality develops within a social environment.

Family, culture, religion, and media may shape beliefs about:

  • Which relationships are acceptable
  • The purpose of sex
  • Gender roles within relationships
  • Modesty and promiscuity
  • Desire and attraction
  • Monogamy
  • LGB identities
  • When sexuality and sexual experiences should be discussed

These influences aren’t automatically harmful. They can provide meaning, values, community, and guidance. However, conflict may develop when a person’s sexuality doesn’t fit the expectations of people who are important to them.

Someone may feel forced to choose between:

  • Honesty and acceptance
  • Sexuality and faith
  • Personal fulfillment and family belonging
  • Safety and disclosure

These conflicts rarely have simple solutions. Working with clients in these situations involves helping the person examine which beliefs they hold, which were imposed, and whether different parts of their life can coexist without requiring rejecting authentic aspects of themselves.

Key Takeaways

  • Sexuality includes attraction, desire, intimacy, relationships, behavior, values, orientation, and self-understanding.
  • Sexual orientation describes patterns of emotional, romantic, or sexual attraction.
  • Sexual, romantic, emotional, physical, and aesthetic attraction may overlap but are not identical.
  • Attraction, behavior, and identity are related but distinct.
  • Sexual orientation and gender identity are different parts of a person’s experience.
  • Labels can provide clarity, validation, belonging, and community.
  • Some people experience sexuality as stable, while others experience fluidity or changing self-understanding.
  • Heteronormativity treats heterosexuality as the expected default and has a significant impact on all of society.
  • Culture, family, religion, and media shape how people understand sexuality.

🧠 Your Mental Playbook

One Thing to Remember

Your sexuality doesn’t need to fit a stereotype, follow a timeline, or be proven through experience to be valid.

Reflect

Consider how you developed your understanding of sexuality.

Ask yourself:

  • What messages did I receive growing up about attraction, relationships, and sexual orientation?
  • Was heterosexuality presented as one possibility or as the only expected possibility?
  • Which sexual orientations were represented positively?
  • Which were stereotyped, ignored, or criticized?
  • Do I confuse someone’s behavior with the totality of their orientation?
  • How have family, religion, culture, or media influenced my beliefs?
  • Which beliefs reflect my current values, and which have I never examined?

Put It Into Practice

Complete a Sexuality Understanding Map.

Divide a page into four sections.

1. Messages I Learned

Write down the messages you received about sexuality.

Examples:

  • “Everyone is expected to be straight.”
  • “Sex should never be discussed.”
  • “Bisexual people are confused.”
  • “Certain relationships are more acceptable than others.”

2. What I Actually Believe

For each message, ask:

“Do I still believe this—and why?”

Separate inherited expectations from beliefs you have consciously chosen.

3. What I Understand About Myself

Without forcing certainty, write what currently feels true regarding:

  • Attraction
  • Romantic interest
  • Relationships
  • Values as it relates to sexuality
  • Labels

4. What I Want to Explore

Choose one question that deserves curiosity rather than judgment.

For example:

  • “Which relationships allow me to feel most emotionally connected?”
  • “Am I using a label because it fits me or because it feels safest?”
  • “What stereotypes have made it harder to understand myself or others?”
  • “What would self-acceptance look like?”
