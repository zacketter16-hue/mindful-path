2 Evolutionary Issues with Motivation

Introduction

For a topic so widely discussed, motivation is very misunderstood. Many think motivation reflects passion, commitment, character, or work ethic.

They think:

“If I really cared, I would feel motivated.”

“If this goal mattered enough, I would want to work on it.”

“Motivated people don’t have to force themselves to do something.”

This creates unnecessary shame. Motivation isn’t a permanent personality trait. It’s a temporary psychological state that changes with energy, mood, stress, confidence, rewards, and circumstances.

You can genuinely care about a goal and still not feel like acting on it. You can be a driven, hard-working person and simply not “have it” some days.

Humans are naturally drawn toward immediate comfort and reward, and many meaningful goals require effort now for benefits that may not appear until much later. Because of this, good habits, long-term goals, and difficult tasks are inherently difficult to get motivated for.

Throughout the Mental Fitness Lounge, you will repeatedly see one central principle:

Action often creates motivation - not the other way around.

This Playbook will not repeat that idea in every section. Instead, it will help you understand why motivation disappears and what your low motivation may be telling you.

Bad Habits are So Much Easier

Long-term goals often involve delayed rewards.

You may need to:

  • Exercise for months before seeing major physical changes
  • Make/save/invest money for years before gaining financial freedom
  • Practice repeatedly before becoming skilled
  • Complete difficult work before receiving recognition
  • Change habits before life feels meaningfully different

Meanwhile, vices and distractions offer immediate rewards.

Your phone, television, food, entertainment, and other forms of escape can improve how you feel within seconds.

This creates a natural conflict:

The meaningful choice often rewards you later. The easier choice rewards you now.

Knowing what to do doesn’t automatically make the delayed reward feel emotionally powerful enough in the moment. The immediate option is visible and certain. The future benefit may feel distant and abstract.

This is why people can sincerely want a result while repeatedly avoiding the actions required to get it.

Your Brain Wants to Conserve Energy

There are 3 basic functions of human behavior: seeking something we want, avoiding something we don’t want, and conserving energy.

Complex tasks that would demand motivation also require attention, uncertainty, planning, frustration tolerance, and the possibility of failure. Your brain naturally notices these costs, and when the task feels too difficult or overwhelming, creates resistance.

You may suddenly feel drawn toward:

  • Checking your phone
  • Organizing something unimportant
  • Eating
  • Watching another episode
  • Masturbating
  • Taking a nap
  • Completing an easier task

Avoidance isn’t always conscious – it can appear as a sudden loss of interest, fatigue, or the belief that now is not the right time.

Not mean every action should be forced, but resistance isn’t reliable evidence that the task is wrong or impossible.

Motivation Loves Certainty

Motivation increases when people know:

  • What to do
  • How to begin
  • What progress looks like
  • Whether success is realistic
  • When the task will end

It often falls when the goal is vague.

Compare:

“I need to get my life together.”

with:

“I will spend fifteen minutes responding to emails today.”

The first goal is emotionally heavy and difficult to begin.

The second provides a clear action that can be done immediately.

A vague goal requires the brain to make several decisions before any work begins. That extra mental effort creates procrastination.

Sometimes low motivation isn’t a desire problem, it’s a clarity problem.

Starting Feels Harder Than Continuing

The beginning of a task contains the greatest amount of psychological friction.

Before starting, you might be imagining the entire project:

  • The time it will take
  • The effort involved
  • Everything that could go wrong
  • How far you still have to go
  • Whether the result will be good enough

Once you begin, attention shifts from imagining the task to doing it. That’s why people sometimes avoid something for hours and then discover it was manageable once started.

“Sometimes that mountain you’ve been climbing is just a grain of sand. And what you’ve been out there searching for forever, is in your hands.” – Carrie Underwood

Progress Creates Motivation

Motivation grows when effort produces visible evidence of progress.

Set yourself up for success. Small wins can create:

  • Confidence
  • Momentum
  • Increased interest
  • A sense of competence
  • Proof of concept that change is possible

The opposite also occurs.

When people work without seeing progress, motivation may decline.

They may begin thinking:

“What is the point?”

“Am I wasting my time?”

This is especially common with goals that produce slow or invisible results. In these instances, progress needs to be measured through behavior rather than immediate outcomes.

For example:

  • Workouts completed rather than pounds lost
  • Applications submitted rather than offers received
  • Money saved rather than complete financial freedom
  • Practice sessions rather than mastery

When the only acceptable reward is the final result, motivation must survive a long period without reinforcement.

Recognizing smaller evidence of progress makes the process easier to continue.

Motivation Is Not a Requirement

One of the most limiting beliefs is:

“I need to feel motivated before I can act.”

You do not.

Obviously you’d prefer to feel motivated, but action is possible even in the presence of boredom, resistance, uncertainty, or low energy.

For example, if you just ate a 5-course meal, and then I put a plate of food in front of you – you might not want to eat it. It’d be helpful to be hungry. But you literally can eat it if you had to.

Separate two different statements:

“I don’t feel motivated.”

and:

“I am unable to take any action.”

They are not the same. Motivation can help action, but it’s not necessary.

Key Takeaways

  • Motivation is a temporary state, not a permanent personality trait.
  • Caring about a goal doesn’t guarantee that you will feel motivated to pursue it.
  • The brain naturally prefers immediate rewards over distant benefits.
  • Effort, uncertainty, and discomfort can create resistance.
  • Vague or overwhelming goals often reduce motivation.
  • Starting often requires more psychological energy than continuing.
  • You do not always need to feel motivated before taking action.

🧠 Your Mental Playbook

One Thing to Remember

Motivation isn’t something you either have or lack. It rises and falls based on how your mind experiences the task in front of you.

When a task feels overwhelming, unclear, unrewarding, or likely to end in failure, motivation drops.

That doesn’t mean you’re lazy or incapable. It means something about the task, the timing, or your current state may need to change.

Reflect

Choose one goal you’ve been avoiding.

Ask yourself:

  • Is the reward immediate or far away?
  • Is the task clear, or does it require several decisions?
  • Am I imagining the entire project instead of the next action?
  • What easier reward usually pulls me away?
  • Have I been waiting to feel motivated before beginning?
  • What evidence of progress could I track?

Low motivation becomes easier to understand when you identify what your mind is moving away from—and what it is moving toward instead.

Put It Into Practice

Complete a Motivation Friction Check.

Write down one task you have been avoiding and answer:

1. The immediate cost

What feels uncomfortable about doing it now?

2. The immediate alternative

What easier or more rewarding activity usually replaces it?

3. The delayed reward

What will completing the task eventually create?

4. The next visible action

Reduce the task to one step that could be completed in ten minutes or less.

Instead of:

“Work on my business.”

try:

“Return a missed call.”

Instead of:

“Get in shape.”

try:

“Put on my shoes and walk for ten minutes.”

“You do not have to see the whole staircase, just take the first step.” — Martin Luther King Jr.
